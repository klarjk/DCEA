## Dripper Geometry and Flow Velocity

**Two effects overlap.** (Source 05)

**① Flow velocity at the bottom (Bernoulli)** — flow velocity is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area. The narrower it becomes toward the bottom, the faster the flow velocity.

The author's worked example (flow rate 2ml/s, top flow velocity assumed to be 1):

| | Bottom/top cross-sectional area | Bottom flow velocity |
|---|---|---|
| Conical (V60) | 50% | 2 |
| Flat-bottom (April) | 80% | 1.25 |

→ The conical's bottom flow velocity is **1.6×** the flat-bottom's. The author's note: "This is a theoretical case and is of course not exact."

**② Temporal persistence of the flow rate** (citing Jonathan Gagne, *The Physics of Filter Coffee*)

- **Conical**: holds a high flow rate well, and **drops sharply in the late phase, so clogging happens readily**
- **Cylindrical · flat-bottom**: the flow rate falls quickly but **it holds a low flow rate well without clogging**

### Summary

| | Conical (V60) | Flat-bottom (Kalita · April · Origami) |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom flow velocity | Fast | Gentle |
| Flow-rate persistence | Holds a high flow rate for a long time, sharp drop in the late phase | Falls quickly, then stable |
| Result | **Much flavor extraction**, bright tone of acidity | **Sweetness-centered** |
| Bypass | Much | Little |
| Clogging | Happens readily | Almost none |

**Connection to the F/C model**: the conical is equipment that pulls a lot of F, the flat-bottom is equipment that leans toward C. For beans with strong flavor the conical is the favorable direction, for beans whose flavor has faded the flat-bottom is.

## The V60's Tendency Toward Greenness

**A phenomenon the author has observed repeatedly** [Observed] (Source 07, 13)

> "If you brew to a high concentration in a dripper like the V60, which has fast flow velocity and holds that fast flow velocity well, **there is a high chance you will taste it.** (Even at cafés said to be famous roasteries, it has been more than once or twice that I was handed coffee that was hot and reeking of greenness when they used a V60.)"

**Hypothesis**: the lower part of the V60's coffee bed is deep, a structure in which gas has difficulty dissipating naturally, so it is likely to end up in the cup. [Hypothesis]

**Prescription**:
- The V60 needs **center-concentrated blooming** to some degree. Or **hollow out the center into a bird's-nest shape**
- On a flat-bottom, blooming may not be necessary at all as long as degassing is sufficient
- Conversely, **beans long past roasting whose aroma has faded** are better brewed **on a conical with no pre-wet** than on a flat-bottom

## How to Create Flow Rate

**Flow rate in filter brewing is proportional to the water level inside the dripper.** (Source 02)

```
Few pours  → large amount poured at once → high water level → high flow rate → much F extracted
Many pours → poured in small splits      → low water level  → low flow rate  → C-centered
```

This is the **main handle** with which the user adjusts flow rate. It is controlled by the number of pours alone, with no flowmeter.

**A high water level has side effects too** — the weight of the water raises pressure, so body↑ clarity↓, and **the bypass water weight increases, so concentration and extraction yield fall**. (Source 16)

**The higher the proportion of center pour**, the faster flow forms and the higher the flow rate goes. (Source 01)

## Pre-wet and Blooming

**Definition of blooming** (the author's own): the phenomenon where, at the spot hollowed out by the water stream, gas from deep down gathers and erupts upward together with water and fines. (Source 16)

- **The longer the pre-wet is held, the lower the bed resistance and the faster the brew speed**
- **The higher the temperature, the greater the effect of the pre-wet**
- Blooming **lifts fines from deep down to the top of the bed**
- The more pre-wet water weight, the slower the total brew time

**In hand drip where the water level never rises above the bed**, blooming keeps happening, so fines are unlikely to travel downward. Hence **the difference in brew speed between the first and second halves is small and there is almost no clogging.**

**April's view** — Patrik Rolf does not see the first pour as blooming, and aims for "wetting as fast and as evenly as possible, with the feeling of extracting from the first pour". Pouring a lot on the first pour at a high flow rate **secures bed resistance**, which prevents the astringency that tends to appear at a low dose. (Source 03)

## Agitation

- **Agitation raises extraction yield while at the same time moving fines downward, raising resistance and slowing brew speed.** It is double-edged
- **Swirling** knocks off the particles stuck to the wall and flattens the bed, **raising consistency**. But the fines that fall settle, so **late-phase resistance can rise**
- **Pour height** is non-monotonic — the higher it goes the greater the impact, but **past a certain height it scatters together with air and the impact actually becomes smaller**

---

## Paper Filters

- The faster the water drains, brew speed↑ → body↓ clarity↑
- The thicker it is, the more fines it catches, so **the probability of clogging is lower**
- **Unbleached (brown) filters tend to have more uniform hole sizes** than bleached white ones
- **Rinsing is almost meaningless in sensory terms.** When brewed together with coffee, the difference is hard to distinguish with human senses. Do it only for the purpose of making the filter sit evenly against the dripper; there is no need for filters that stick well or small single-cup filters

The author's comparison: **Cafec light** has less sourness and is smoother than Hario 02. Brew time is slightly slower, by under 10 s. (Source 01)

---

## Why the Brew Is Cut Short

**The last 10ml takes 10~20 s or more to extract.** In that segment the flow rate is extremely low, so coffee with a collapsed balance mixes in.

It is better to remove the dripper once the target beverage weight is reached. (Source 03)

## Grinder Families and Flavor

**The more flat-burr · unimodal the family, the stronger the flavor; conical-burr and hand-grinder families express flavor weakly and have their strength in texture.** [Observed] (Source 02)

> "Hand grinders, **all of them regardless of type**, give a very weak flavor intensity compared to the P64 when brewed, so for light-roast filter it is not easy to obtain the same satisfaction as the P64 with ordinary recipe adjustments."

**For hand-grinder users, recommend the low-flow-rate · high-TDS direction from the start** (the Stage 3 recipe). Trying to draw flavor out by raising the flow rate on equipment that lacks flavor is a forbidden path in `pitfalls.md`.

---

## Roast Level and Target TDS

The lower the roast level, the more flavor there is, and the more flavor there is, the stronger the stimulus, so **balance is found at a low TDS.** Conversely, when there is little flavor, you have to aim for **a high TDS** to get a cup with good texture. (Source 02)

> "If you measure the TDS of a well-brewed dark-roast cup, it is far higher than a light roast."

---

## Brew Ratio and Flavor (with Grind Size Fixed)

- The higher the brew ratio (1:14~1:15, less water), the **stronger** the flavor
- The lower it is (1:16~1:17, more water), the **weaker** the flavor
- "You can feel a clear difference from as little as 10ml of water weight, so **a scale is not optional but essential**"

**However, if you adjust grind size along with it so that TDS matches, there is no large difference** — "within a range that is not large, there is less difference in flavor than you would think". (Source 02)

---

## The Astringency Limit Line by Grinder

If a drying sensation persists even when the conditions for astringency (uneven extraction · excessive flow rate · insufficient dose) are not present, **raise the brew ratio, pouring less water, and open up the grind size to maintain the concentration.** (Source 01)

On the author's setup (P64 SSP LS):

| Bean | Astringency |
|---|---|
| Medium-light 1:16 | Slightly present |
| Medium-light 1:15 | Almost none |
| Light · Nordic 1:17 | Barely noticeable (but the grind size has to be fine, so lowering the brew ratio is a burden) |

> "I feel that **the appropriate brew ratio at which astringency is not perceived differs from grinder to grinder**."

Do not hand these numbers to the user as they are; guide them with **a method for finding their own limit line**.
