Honestly, that period was also spent completing my own method and experimenting with it,
and once I felt there wouldn't be any more progress on the theoretical side of coffee extraction, my interest dropped sharply and I didn't much feel like writing.
It's also been a while, so I don't remember the earlier installments well.
Skimming back over it, it looks like I was examining how flavor changes by variable,
so rather than rambling on, I think I'll just write this one up as 'in my experience, changing this makes the flavor turn out like this!'
Please excuse it if it doesn't flow smoothly from the previous content.
Among the various variables affecting flavor, I'll organize the fundamental ones first, then the ones that respond relatively well to adjustment, in that order.
(1) Roasting
The lower the roast level, the more flavor there is, and the higher, the less. Naturally.
The more flavor there is, the stronger the stimulus, so balance is achieved at a low TDS, and when there's less, conversely you have to match a high TDS to extract a cup with excellent texture instead of flavor.
That's why Nordic roasts are generally known to be extracted tea-like,
and if you measure the TDS of a well-extracted dark roast cup, it's much higher than a light roast.
(2) Grinder
Generally, flat burr grinders, and grinders/burrs known to be unimodal, tend to have stronger flavor,
while conical and hand-grinder-type grinders have weaker flavor expression and an advantage in texture.
My grinder is mainly the P64 SSP LS, and for hand grinders I use the Comandante, Kinu (Classic, Brew burr), K-Ultra, and X-Lite,
but regardless of type, hand grinders all produce much weaker flavor intensity when extracted compared to the P64, so with a light roast filter coffee it's not easy to get the same satisfaction as the P64 with ordinary recipe adjustments.
(3) Dose
The larger the dose, the stronger the flavor, and the smaller, the weaker.
The dose referred to here is the dose when the brew ratio is all the same.
That is, if you extract 15g:240g, 18g:288g, and 20g:320g all under the same conditions, only varying grind size, to reach the same TDS,
15g has the least flavor and 20g has the most.
Well, since coffee and water increase proportionally, if you think about it purely in terms of concentration, the result should theoretically be similar,
but in practice, that's how it turns out.
It could be that the extraction flow differs depending on whether the dose is large or small,
or it could be that the amount of coffee itself, regardless of the amount of water, affects flavor intensity.
(4) Degassing
Without much need for explanation, coffee that's less time off roast has more flavor,
and coffee that's more time off roast has weaker flavor.
(5) Temperature
While the previous four factors mainly affect only the flavor itself, from the fifth one onward these also affect the actually measured concentration, i.e., TDS.
So because the previous four change the coffee's flavor itself,
if one of those variables changes, it's hard to maintain the same cup through recipe adjustment alone,
whereas conversely the following factors are things you can fine-tune while adjusting TDS — parts the barista can actively engage with.
As temperature rises, flavor intensity and TDS increase together,
and as it falls, they conversely decrease.
(6) Flow rate
Assuming TDS is held constant,
the higher the flow rate, i.e., the shorter the brew time, the greater the flavor intensity,
and the lower the flow rate, i.e., the longer the brew time, the lower the flavor intensity.
In filter extraction, flow rate is proportional to the water level in the dripper,
so the fewer the number of pours, the higher the flow rate, and conversely the more pours it's split into, the lower the flow rate.
Temperature also has a slight effect on flow rate. (The higher the temperature, the slightly higher the flow rate.)
(7) Brew ratio
The lower the brew ratio (e.g., 1:16~17), i.e., the more water, the weaker the flavor,
and the higher it is (e.g., 1:14~1:15), the stronger the flavor.
Even a 10ml difference in water weight is enough to feel a noticeable difference,
so a scale isn't optional — it's essential.
But this is the case when grind size is held constant,
and if you compare results with TDS held the same, i.e., raising the brew ratio (pour less water) but loosening the grind slightly to keep concentration the same, within a not-too-large range there's less flavor difference than you'd expect.
(8) Grind size
I mainly use grind size, unrelated to flavor, to hit the target TDS.
However, if it's too fine, negative sensory notes appear, so there's a limit to how far you can go finer.
I've written up the variables briefly,
but the core content will probably be covered in the next installment, actual recipe adjustment.
