The next series will write up what I found out about how perceived sensory characteristics change with flow velocity.
I believe nothing in the world is absolute.
Even that sun in the sky has a lifespan, and even time and space act relatively depending on gravity.
Meaning nothing stays unchanged and lasts forever.
(My apologies to those who believe in God.)
I think the same is true for coffee.
The mainstream research trend in the coffee world is Even Extraction — the idea that if the extraction is perfectly even, you get delicious coffee even at higher concentration and yield.
I think a perfectly even extraction can't exist, and that given the structure of human senses, there's an appropriate range of concentration and yield that comes across as pleasant.
That said, the better the texture, the more a smooth quality can reduce harshness and let it tolerate a higher concentration and yield.
(If you apply an appropriate Americano concentration setting straight to a milk drink, it usually tastes flat and washed-out.
Conversely, if you apply an appropriate milk-drink setting to an Americano, it usually tastes too strong.)
(I think the reason cupping usually feels less harsh than filter brewing and tastes good even at a higher yield is, along with the above reason, that not much flavor compound is extracted.)
So when a situation we normally call under-extraction (sour or greenish) tastes harsh and unpleasant,
I think it's not because something was extracted too little, but a phenomenon that appears because flavor compounds were extracted excessively.
If compounds had been extracted too little, the normal result should be a sensory note that's flat and washed-out or somehow empty, not a harsh one.
Immersion has no flow, so extraction of these flavor compounds is lower,
while with percolation, the faster the flow, the more the extraction of light flavor compounds increases,
and once the concentration rises past a certain range, it starts to feel negative.
Because these flavor compounds don't register on a refractometer and are volatile,
they're not easy to experiment on or prove with scientific thinking.
I was lucky enough to have been able to run experimental brews using a Decent machine, mechanically controlling the flow rate.
(To rule out the influence of other factors, I used a grind size around that of filter coffee, minimizing the influence of pressure and grind size as much as possible.)
Accordingly, briefly organizing what I found out empirically,
the first post will lay out a hypothesis citing data from the Brewing Control Chart,
the second will be an actual brewing log examining whether the first post's content holds up,
and the third will be a post organizing the process of actually applying it to filter brewing and espresso.
