# An Experiment on Flavor Change Driven by Flow Velocity

In this post, I'll take what I laid out in the previous post — Reflections on Flavor Change Driven by Flow Velocity —,
and spend some time actually applying it: varying the flow rate on a Decent machine and seeing how the sensory changes play out in practice.
I'll write this assuming readers who are not Decent users, centering the content on general points, and mark the parts meant for Decent users differently so they're easy to spot and skip quickly.
(1) Sprover
[image: https://postfiles.pstatic.net/MjAyMzA5MDdfMjcz/MDAxNjk0MDQ2MTY2NDM4.yIhmh5CGaBwj_LVbc45169GybzR3ZSyAas4WTugtYjgg.fTg5f441c2hy1_gv9WeNPO_vVzAhi8DOAnetzJpLVLIg.PNG.klar201/image.png?type=w580]
I've introduced the Sprover profile using a WAFO basket before as well.
It was a brew using a grind size similar to actual filter brewing, with a blooming phase, targeting a similar concentration.
After brewing it many times, oddly enough, astringency showed up more often than with regular brewing, even though the pressure didn't rise very much.
So I figured it was just a structural limitation and didn't use it for quite a while.
But once I started thinking about flow velocity and extraction again, I began seriously trying various things by changing the flow rate and profiling.
(2) The U90 Profile
So I started rebuilding the profile from scratch.
The new version had two core goals.
The first was to raise the flow rate early on to bring out as much flavor as possible, while setting an upper cap to eliminate astringency,
and the second was to use, instead of a flat profile, a profile where the flow rate starts high and gradually decreases like actual brewing, to prevent over-extracting the flavor compounds that run out later on.
[image: https://postfiles.pstatic.net/MjAyMzA5MDdfMTY0/MDAxNjk0MDUzNzA1NzEy.FH81p9P_qT8MAG_U4LzbjAJg2crx5h-oGFxFU8aDx7cg.m3zd8UUZBvOrcpAC5AvepnT-yEwJ0kw7AQxtzMDiJf8g.PNG.klar201/image.png?type=w580]
For those who haven't seen the earlier post about the previous version of the Sprover profile, let me first briefly explain:
it's a way of brewing like a non-bypass brewing machine, using a Decent machine and a low-resistance, high-yield basket while controlling the flow rate.
In the earlier version I used the WAFO basket, which has even lower resistance, but I decided basket shape mattered more and switched to a Unifilter basket,
and likewise, I used two paper filters to remove the fat-soluble compounds that get extracted whenever a bit of pressure builds up, aiming to secure a clean feel like regular filter brewing.
And since blooming is meant to extract flavor with a high flow rate, I judged it unnecessary and decided to remove it.
I named it U90, playing off Hario's V60 — it's U-shaped and at 90 degrees.
The brew has three phases in total: the first 30 seconds extract flavor as much as possible with a high flow rate,
from the next 30 seconds the flow rate gradually decreases at a lower level,
and the final phase proceeds at a constant minimum flow rate until it reaches the target weight.
Looking at the record above, astringency was still severe.
I judged that the early flow rate was still too high, and decided to lower it further.
(For reference, judge the flow rate by looking at the brown line, which is the actual brew flow rate.)
※ Information on machine flow rate (blue line) and brew flow rate (brown line) for Decent users
The way Decent calculates and displays flow rate on the graph has its coefficient tuned to resistance around the level of espresso.
So when it's used for a turbo shot or as a brewing machine, where the resistance is very different and the flow velocity is fast, the machine's flow rate reads about 30–50% lower than the actual flow rate.
By Bernoulli's theorem, the machine's flow rate should equal the flow rate during the brew,
so the brew flow rate being measured in real time through the scale is the accurate data,
and therefore you should judge the flow rate based on the brown brew-flow-rate line.
[image: https://postfiles.pstatic.net/MjAyMzA5MDdfMjI2/MDAxNjk0MDgzOTcyMDgy.3_QYBu9x62Gca-RB2Kk7xB4onxURN-6esMmYGlqw0Kkg.s4CzDTn438vGfku_PatD3gpuyOOcjn4iYXZTARf2-B4g.PNG.klar201/image.png?type=w580]
Setting the peak early flow rate to 4 mlps or below as shown above, I couldn't perceive any astringency at all.
Even so, there was still greenness — maybe because flavor compound extraction was still high — and the first sip felt uncomfortable because the temperature was high.
(By the time I'd drunk about half and it had cooled, the greenness was gone and it was pleasant to drink.)
Since lowering the temperature would also weaken flavor expression, I left every other variable as is and lowered only the temperature by 3°C — the greenness disappeared and I got a decent result.
But I judged that this Honduras Parainema's main flavor was a grain-syrup-like sweetness, a coffee that didn't pair all that well with strong acidity, so I tried lowering the early flow rate even further.
[image: https://postfiles.pstatic.net/MjAyMzA5MDlfMjcg/MDAxNjk0MjI0MDY3NTg5.xw4MIqZdLV8kz_UM61lJxTgYFh8gkHNJvr8d3_xCpZIg.29968n8eqkQCfTLcmxhLDmHOGRVT4mhF9RfjHAANH2cg.PNG.klar201/image.png?type=w580]
Lowering the brew flow rate to around 3 mlps produced a delicious cup with moderate acidity early on and, as it cooled toward the end, very good sweetness.
Since grain syrup doesn't pair well with acidity, it might be worth lowering it all the way to 2 mlps just to emphasize the sweetness alone.
And this is something I came to notice while brewing:
the higher the flow rate I brewed with, the more intense the stimulus felt even at a lower concentration,
and the lower the flow rate, the less strongly the stimulus came through even at a higher concentration.
So for a high-flow-rate brew meant to bring out acidity, I aimed for a target TDS of around 1.2–1.25%,
and for a sweetness-focused, low-flow-rate brew, a target TDS of around 1.3–1.35% felt appropriate to me.
(This might be why a very slow brew or an immersion brew can taste good without a harsh stimulus even at a TDS above 1.4%.)
※ A starting guide for Decent users who want to try this themselves
Start with a grind size similar to regular filter coffee.
My brew ratio setting was 15.5 g:210 g.
Be sure to use two paper filters with a high-yield basket.
I recommend doing WDT, and for tamping I recommend just a light tamp using roughly the tamper's own weight, treating it more like leveling.
I don't recommend a puck screen.
If you split the early flow-rate setting into three phases, make it a profile that decreases in sequence: 1.2–2.2 mlps → 1.2 mlps → 0.9 mlps. (Set the temperature similarly as well.)
The flow-rate figures in #6 above are just a guide — in practice, judge and adjust using the brew flow rate (brown line).
In profile editing, be sure to turn on the water tank preheat function and set it to 30°C, or the temperature will drop later in the brew.
Since TDS doesn't change much with flow rate, mainly adjust the grind size to hit the concentration you want.
(3) Summary
By varying mainly the early flow rate, I found that a high flow rate emphasizes acidity and flavor, while having no high-flow-rate section leads to a sweetness-centered result.
With a high-flow-rate brew, since flavor compounds run high, the stimulus feels strong even when the actual TDS is low, so I needed to loosen the grind and lower the concentration accordingly.
By adjusting the flow rate appropriately for a given coffee's flavor notes, I was able to maximize the expression of that cup's notes.
If the flow rate is very high in some section, astringency shows up; if it's high overall, negative sensory notes centered on greenness show up.
(4) Closing
The next post will take what I've learned about varying the flow rate and apply it to brewing with an actual dripper.
Since I've only recently started checking flow rate with manual brewing, it'll probably take some time before the data builds up.
I hope everyone enjoys their coffee life.
